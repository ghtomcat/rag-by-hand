A Visitor's Guide to Kestrel Island

Kestrel Island is a small island in the cold northern sea, about eleven nautical miles
off the mainland harbour town of Port Alda. It is roughly six kilometres long and two
kilometres wide at its broadest point, and it rises from a low sandy shore in the south
to steep cliffs in the north. Around 412 people live on the island all year round, most
of them in the single village of Hollin, which curves around a sheltered harbour on the
east coast. This guide is written for first-time visitors. It explains how to get to
the island, where to stay, what to see, and the few local rules that every visitor is
expected to follow.

The island takes its name from the kestrels that once nested in large numbers on the
northern cliffs. Only a few pairs remain today, but the bird appears on the village
sign, on the ferry's funnel and on almost every postcard sold in the harbour shop.
Islanders call themselves Hollin folk, regardless of where on the island they live,
and they call everyone from the mainland "over-water people", usually with a smile.

The first permanent settlers arrived in 1742. They were salt makers from the mainland
who boiled seawater in large iron pans over peat fires and sold the salt in Port Alda.
The remains of their salt pans can still be seen at Brine Cove on the south-west shore,
where a row of low stone walls marks the outline of the old boiling houses. Salt making
ended in 1868, when cheaper salt from mines on the mainland made the island's trade
unprofitable. Most families then turned to fishing, especially for mackerel and herring,
and fishing remained the main source of income until the 1970s.

The lighthouse on Gannet Head, the northernmost point of the island, was built in 1891
to a design by the engineer Ilse Marrow. It was the first lighthouse in the region to
use a rotating lens floating in a bath of mercury, which allowed the heavy lens to turn
smoothly with very little force. The light was operated by resident keepers until 1979,
when it was automated. The three keepers' cottages beside the tower are now a small
museum about the lighthouse and the families who lived there. The tower itself is open
to visitors only on Lantern Night, described further below.

Getting to Kestrel Island is only possible by sea. There is no airport and no landing
strip, although an emergency helicopter can land on the football pitch behind the
school. The ferry Merle Ann sails from Port Alda to Hollin three times a day, leaving
the mainland at 7:40, 12:15 and 17:30. The crossing takes about fifty-five minutes in
normal weather. The return sailings leave Hollin at 9:00, 13:45 and 19:00. On Sundays
there is no morning sailing, so the first ferry of the day leaves Port Alda at 12:15.

A return ticket for an adult costs 24 crowns, and a return ticket for a child under
twelve costs 12 crowns. Children under four travel free. Bicycles can be taken on the
ferry for an extra 5 crowns each way, but there is space for only eight bicycles per
sailing, so it is worth arriving early in summer. Tickets are sold at the harbour office
in Port Alda and on board, but the ticket desk on board accepts cash only. Dogs travel
free but must be kept on a lead on the open deck.

The ferry does not sail when the wind is stronger than force 7 on the Beaufort scale.
In autumn and winter this can happen several times a month, and cancellations of two or
three days in a row are not unusual. The ferry company posts cancellations on the board
outside the harbour office in Port Alda and announces them on the local radio station
at 6:30 each morning. Visitors in the colder months should always plan an extra day at
the end of their trip in case they cannot get back to the mainland on time.

Private cars are not allowed on Kestrel Island. The only motor vehicles are the delivery
van that meets the ferry, the doctor's small electric car, two tractors and the island's
fire engine. Visitors get around on foot or by bicycle. The Harbour Cycle Shed, next to
the ferry landing in Hollin, rents out about forty bicycles, including a few with child
seats and two electric bicycles for people who find the northern hills too steep. A
bicycle costs 8 crowns per day, and the shop is open every day from 8:30 to 18:00
between April and October. In winter it opens only when the ferry arrives.

The village of Hollin has one shop, one pub, one café and a small post office. The shop,
Teague's General Store, sells groceries, fishing tackle, maps, rain gear and almost
anything else a visitor might forget to bring. It is open Monday to Saturday from 8:00
to 18:00 and on Sundays from 12:00 to 15:00. Fresh bread and milk arrive on the morning
ferry, so on days when the ferry is cancelled the shelves can empty quickly. There is no
bank on the island and no cash machine. Most places accept cards, but the ferry desk,
the honesty boxes at the farm stalls and the summer boat trips accept cash only, so
visitors should bring enough cash for their whole stay.

There are three places to stay on the island. The Anchor Inn in Hollin has eight rooms
above the pub and serves breakfast to its guests. The Gull House, a guest house run by
the Ferrand family on the hill above the harbour, has five rooms and is known for its
large breakfasts and its view of the harbour at sunrise. For visitors on a smaller
budget, there is a campsite at Saltgrass Meadow, a fifteen-minute walk south of the
village, with space for about thirty tents, hot showers and a shelter for cooking in
bad weather. The campsite is open from May to September only. In July and August all
three places are usually full, so booking several months ahead is strongly advised.

The best known walk on the island is the Cliff Ring, a marked path of fourteen
kilometres that follows the whole coastline. Most walkers need between four and five
hours, starting and finishing in Hollin. The path is marked with white posts topped with
a painted kestrel. The northern section, between Gannet Head and Grey Point, runs close
to the cliff edge and can be dangerous in strong wind or fog. Walkers are asked to stay
on the marked path at all times, especially between April and August, when birds are
nesting on the cliffs, and to keep dogs on a lead for the whole walk.

Kestrel Island is well known for its seabirds. Puffins nest in burrows on the northern
cliffs from April to early August, and the best place to watch them is the viewing
platform near Gannet Head, where they often stand only a few metres away. Guillemots,
razorbills and fulmars also nest on the cliffs, and gannets can be seen diving for fish
offshore. A colony of grey seals lives at Grey Point, on the north-west coast. The seals
give birth to their pups between October and December, and during that time the beach
below Grey Point is closed to visitors so that the mothers are not disturbed. The seals
can still be watched from the cliff path above.

In summer, a small boat called the Skua takes visitors around the northern cliffs to see
the seabirds from the water. The trip lasts about ninety minutes and leaves the harbour
at 10:00 and 14:30 from June to August, weather permitting. Places are limited to
twelve people, and tickets are sold at the Anchor Inn on the day before the trip. The
skipper, Rory Ferrand, has worked on the island's waters for more than thirty years and
knows the name of nearly every rock along the coast.

The Lighthouse Museum in the old keepers' cottages tells the story of the light on
Gannet Head and of the families who kept it burning. Among the objects on display are
the original keepers' logbooks, a model of the mercury bath that carries the lens, and a
collection of photographs from 1900 to 1979. The museum is open from Tuesday to Sunday,
11:00 to 16:00, between May and September. Entry costs 6 crowns for adults and is free
for children. In the rest of the year it can be opened by arrangement with the island
council.

The most important event of the year is Lantern Night, held on the first Saturday of
September. It began in the 1890s as a celebration for the new lighthouse and has been
held almost every year since. In the evening, islanders and visitors walk in a long
procession from the harbour to Gannet Head, each carrying a paper lantern. When the
procession reaches the lighthouse, the lamp is switched off for exactly one minute,
and the only light on the headland comes from the lanterns. This is also the only night
of the year when visitors may climb the 127 steps to the top of the tower. After the
procession, there is music and food in the harbour until late. Lanterns can be bought at
Teague's General Store or made at a workshop in the school hall on the afternoon before.

Food on the island is simple and mostly local. The pub at the Anchor Inn serves fish
landed the same morning, and its smoked mackerel, smoked over beech wood in a small
smokehouse behind the pub, is famous across the region. The Harbour Café is the place to
try brack cake, a dark, heavy fruit cake made with tea, dried fruit and a spoonful of
the island's sea salt. It is traditionally eaten on Lantern Night, but the café sells
it all year. Several farms sell eggs, honey and vegetables from small roadside stalls
that work on trust: visitors take what they need and leave the money in a box.

Fresh water is precious on Kestrel Island. All drinking water comes from a single
reservoir in the hills above Hollin, filled by rain, and in dry summers the island
council sometimes asks everyone to save water. Visitors are asked to take short
showers and not to wash cars, bicycles or boats with tap water. Electricity comes from
three wind turbines on the western ridge and from solar panels on the school roof,
with a diesel generator as a backup when the wind is calm for several days. Mobile
phone reception is good in Hollin but weak or absent on the northern cliffs, so walkers
should not rely on their phones in an emergency.

There is no hospital on the island. A doctor holds a clinic in Hollin on Tuesdays and
Thursdays from 9:00 to 12:00, and a nurse lives in the village and can be reached at any
time in an emergency. Serious cases are flown to the mainland by helicopter. Visitors
who take regular medicine should bring enough for their whole stay, plus a few extra
days in case the ferry is cancelled, because there is no pharmacy on the island.

The island has a few rules that visitors are asked to respect. Fires are not allowed
anywhere outside the campsite's cooking shelter. Camping outside Saltgrass Meadow is not
permitted. Drones may not be flown near the northern cliffs at any time of year, because
they frighten the nesting birds. Shells and stones may not be taken from Brine Cove,
where they form part of the protected remains of the old salt works. And all rubbish must
be taken back to the mainland or put in the recycling point at the harbour, since the
island has no landfill of its own.

Kestrel Island is governed by an island council of seven members, elected every four
years by everyone who lives on the island. The council meets on the first Monday of each
month in the school hall, and its meetings are open to visitors. The island school
teaches about thirty children up to the age of twelve. Older children take the morning
ferry to the secondary school in Port Alda and stay with families there during the week
when the weather is bad.

The best time to visit depends on what you want to see. May to August brings long days,
puffins and the most reliable ferry service, but also the most visitors. September is
quieter, often still mild, and includes Lantern Night. From October to December the
seals have their pups at Grey Point, but storms are more frequent and the ferry is more
often cancelled. In January and February many places close, and the island belongs
mostly to the people who live there. Whatever the season, visitors should bring warm
clothes, rain gear and good walking shoes, because the weather can change from sunshine
to a cold, wet wind in less than an hour.
